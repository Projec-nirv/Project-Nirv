Description:
Analysis of CAC, post-view conversion, contribution LTV, and marketplace memory in Indian fashion marketplaces, using Myntra, Nykaa Fashion, Meesho, Flipkart, Amazon India, Reliance Retail, TMRW, and Trent as operating references.

# Core Thesis
A new Indian fashion marketplace does not fail simply because CAC is high. It fails when acquired customers do not create enough retained contribution and marketplace memory to make CAC recoverable. The strategic test is whether the first 150,000 monthly orders reduce future paid-acquisition dependence.

# Key Findings

## Finding 1: CAC Is A Symptom
- CAC reflects missing trust, weak habit, weak supply control, and weak repeat behavior.
- A low first-order CAC is not enough if the customer must be reacquired or subsidized again.
- The relevant question is whether CAC buys a customer the marketplace can use again.

## Finding 2: Marketplace Memory Decides Compounding
- Marketplace memory appears through repeat customers, reliable seller demand, creator conversion, CRM response, lower return leakage, and traffic quality valuable enough for sellers to pay for visibility.
- Without memory, the marketplace remains a paid acquisition surface.
- With memory, CAC becomes the cost of adding a customer to a system that can reuse demand.

## Finding 3: Post-View Conversion Needs Incrementality
- Post-view conversion can overstate acquisition quality if viewability and incrementality are not stress-tested.
- The report models post-view CAC using viewability and incrementality adjustments.
- Attribution quality matters because fashion discovery is heavily content-led.

## Finding 4: Contribution LTV Must Clear CAC
- The report uses PVC-LTV/CAC near or above 3x as a margin-of-safety hurdle.
- Contribution per retained order, repeat depth, returns, discounts, and AOV determine the CAC ceiling.
- Gross orders are not the unit of truth; retained contribution is.

## Finding 5: Product Architecture Sets The CAC Ceiling
- Product type, price point, return risk, logistics burden, and category mix determine how much paid acquisition the model can absorb.
- Low AOV fashion can convert cheaply but leaves little room for CAC errors.
- Broad catalog expansion before cohort proof can scale the problem instead of solving it.

# Main Sections

## Executive Summary
- Defines marketplace memory and PVC-LTV.
- Frames the report around CAC recovery, retained contribution, and dependency reduction.

## Section 1: The Marketplace That Pays Before It Sells
- Establishes the Indian evidence base and operating references.
- Explains why acquisition cost must be judged by retained behavior.

## Section 2: The Same Checkout Hides Different Machines
- Compares Myntra, Nykaa Fashion, Meesho, Flipkart, Amazon India, Reliance Retail/AJIO, TMRW, and Trent/Zudio.
- Shows that similar consumer journeys can hide different economic engines.

## Section 3: CAC Is Not A Number. It Is A Symptom
- Links CAC pressure to public filings, trust gaps, category economics, and post-view conversion quality.
- Introduces the post-view CAC formula and early viability tests.

## Section 4: The Customer Has To Carry The Acquisition Cost
- Models contribution, retained orders, payback depth, and CAC ceiling.
- Uses a 3x PVC-LTV/CAC hurdle for margin of safety.

## Section 5: Growth Only Matters When Dependence Falls
- Compares paths to 150,000 monthly orders.
- Tests whether paid acquisition share falls as repeat, CRM, direct, and organic behavior rises.

## Section 6: Marketing Has To Become An Operating System
- Separates creator-led, founder-led, brand-led, paid, CRM, affiliate, and AI-assisted loops.
- Argues that channels matter only if they improve the economics of the next order.

## Section 7: The Product Sets The CAC Ceiling
- Examines AOV, GST price boundaries, returns, brand type, and logistics burden.
- Shows why product architecture determines acquisition tolerance.

## Section 8: The Model Has To Be Falsifiable
- Defines operating scorecards, incrementality tests, cohort payback tests, and category-level measurement.
- Treats gross orders as insufficient evidence.

## Section 9: The First Engine Decides The Company
- Tests three marketplace versions: discount-led, creator-led, and curated mid-market.
- Recommends curated supply, creator-led discovery, paid amplification of validated cohorts, CRM, and category-level contribution tracking.

## Section 10: Conclusion
- Frames the first 150,000 monthly orders as an experiment in dependency reduction.
- Recommends judging the marketplace by whether the second order costs less to create.
