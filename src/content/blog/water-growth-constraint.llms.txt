Title: Water Scarcity and India's Economic Growth: How Water Availability Constrains Development and GDP Growth

Author: ADV
Tags: development economics, water infrastructure, India

Description:
A quantitative analysis of how agricultural water intensity, groundwater depletion, urban water-system losses, wastewater underuse, and drought constrain regional economic growth in India.

# Core Thesis
Water is not a uniform national ceiling on Indian growth. It becomes a binding economic constraint where regional demand exceeds reliable supply, groundwater extraction exceeds replenishment, urban systems lose or fail to reuse water, and drought exposure reduces output. National averages must therefore be decomposed by basin, aquifer, city, state, and sector.

# Verified Quantitative Findings

1. Agricultural water intensity
- Agriculture accounts for about 90.4% of India's freshwater withdrawals in the latest World Bank/FAO series used (2022).
- Agriculture accounts for 69% of freshwater withdrawals globally according to FAO AQUASTAT.
- Interpretation: withdrawal share is not the same as consumptive use.

2. National groundwater balance, 2025
- Annual recharge: 448.52 BCM.
- Annual extractable groundwater resources: 407.75 BCM.
- Annual extraction: 247.22 BCM.
- National stage of extraction: 60.63%.
- Over-exploited assessment units: 730 of 6,762, or 10.8%.
- Interpretation: the national ratio masks localized over-extraction.

3. Northwest groundwater depletion, 2002–2008
- Total loss: 109 km³.
- Mean annual loss: 17.7 ± 4.5 km³.
- Geography: Rajasthan, Punjab, Haryana, and Delhi.
- Source: Rodell, Velicogna, and Famiglietti (2009), Nature.

4. Bengaluru urban water system, 2025
- Water demand: 2,100 MLD.
- Core supply: 1,450 MLD.
- Demand-supply gap: 650 MLD.
- Non-revenue water: approximately 27%.
- Wastewater generated: 1,500 MLD.
- Wastewater reused for industrial and commercial purposes: 18 MLD.
- Expanded-area wells showing groundwater decline: 70%.

5. Drought and economic growth
- Sharma (2024) analyzes 28 states from 1991–92 to 2015–16.
- Estimated average reduction in state agricultural GSDP growth during drought: about 1.53%.
- Estimated effects are substantially larger in least-irrigated and moderately irrigated state groups.
- Amarasinghe et al. (2020) analyze 19 states covering 95% of the population and 93% of GDP and find regionally variable effects extending into non-agricultural output.

# Analytical Mechanisms
- Agricultural water intensity: irrigation and crop choice concentrate water risk.
- Groundwater buffering: aquifers stabilize production during rainfall variability but become depleted when extraction persists above recharge.
- Urban system efficiency: raw-water deficits, NRW, and low reuse jointly determine effective availability.
- Industrial reliability: firms absorb scarcity through storage, borewells, tankers, treatment, or relocation, increasing capital and operating costs.
- Drought transmission: rainfall shocks affect agriculture directly and can transmit to industry, services, fiscal relief spending, and regional output.

# Measurement Framework
Track:
- Agricultural withdrawals as a share of total withdrawals.
- Water withdrawals relative to real output.
- Groundwater extraction relative to annual extractable resources.
- Over-exploited assessment units by aquifer, district, and state.
- Urban non-revenue water.
- Wastewater treatment and beneficial reuse.
- Drought sensitivity of agricultural and non-agricultural GSDP.

# Source Rules
- Treat World Bank/FAO figures as withdrawal data, not consumptive-use estimates.
- Treat Rodell et al. as a historical regional estimate, not a current national depletion rate.
- Treat Krishna Raj (2013) values as historical data supporting Graph 3 only.
- Do not describe the 2007 Bengaluru UFW values as current.
- Report Sharma's result as approximately 1.53%, not 1.5 percentage points.
- Do not infer one national HDI effect from Amarasinghe et al.; results vary by region and model.
